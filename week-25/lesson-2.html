From Cash and Carry to Pearl Harbor

📖 TODAY'S LESSON

When Germany invaded Poland in September 1939, Roosevelt told the country that it could not expect him to stay neutral in thought, but he promised to keep it out of the fighting. He also asked Congress to change the Neutrality Actsnoo-TRAL-ih-tee aktsLaws passed in the 1930s that banned arms sales to countries at war and kept Americans out of war zones.. After weeks of bitter debate, Congress agreed in November, the Senate by 63 votes to 30 and the House by 243 to 181, and the ban on selling weapons was lifted. The new law allowed Britain and France to buy weapons from the United States, but on one condition. They had to pay in full and carry the goods away in their own ships. This policy was called cash and carrykash and KAIR-eeA policy that let nations at war buy goods in the United States only if they paid in full and carried the goods away in their own ships.. A form of it had been part of the 1937 law, but it had run out in May 1939. Germany had no way to send its own ships across the Atlantic to pick up American goods. Its supporters claimed that it would keep Americans out of danger. Its real effect was to help the Allied PowersAL-yd POW-urzThe nations that fought the Axis in World War II, led by Britain, the Soviet Union, and the United States., the nations fighting Germany, because the British navy controlled the Atlantic.

For the next several months, the fighting in the west was so quiet that newspapers called it the Phony War. The war then changed very quickly. In April 1940, Germany overran Denmark and Norway. In May, it struck Belgium, the Netherlands, and France. Within six weeks, France, which many people thought had the strongest army in Europe, had surrendered. German tanks had come through the Ardennes forest, which the French had thought too thick for armor, and went around the strong forts on France's border. Hitler made the French sign the surrender in the same railway car in which Germany had signed in 1918. Britain's army escaped from the beach at Dunkirk, but it left most of its heavy weapons behind. Fishing boats and pleasure craft joined navy ships in crossing the Channel to carry off more than three hundred thousand soldiers. The British prime minister, Winston ChurchillWIN-stun CHUR-chilThe prime minister of Britain from 1940, who led his country through most of the war., had replaced Chamberlain on the day the attack began. He refused to make peace. He told Parliament that Britain would fight on the beaches and would never surrender. That summer, German planes tried to destroy the Royal Air Force and then began bombing British cities. On American radios, the reporter Edward R. Murrow described the bombing of London from a rooftop, and listeners at home heard the sirens. Britain stood almost alone. The long policy of appeasementuh-PEEZ-mentThe policy of giving in to an aggressor's demands in the hope of keeping the peace. had failed.

Americans were shocked. In September 1940, Congress passed the Selective Service Actsih-LEK-tiv SUR-vis aktThe 1940 law that created the first peacetime draft in American history., the first peacetime draft in American history. Men between twenty-one and thirty-five had to register. About sixteen million did so in October, and in a drawing in Washington, Secretary of War Henry Stimson, blindfolded, pulled the first number from a glass bowl. The draftees were to serve for one year. In August 1941, as that year neared its end, Congress extended it by a single vote in the House, 203 to 202. Back in September 1940, Roosevelt had traded fifty old navy destroyers to Britain in return for the use of British bases in the Western Hemisphere. The ships were old, and Britain gave the United States leases of ninety-nine years on bases in Newfoundland and the Caribbean. He did this on his own authority, without a vote of Congress, and some people said that he had gone too far.

That fall was also an election year. Since George Washington, no president had served more than two terms. Ulysses Grant and Theodore Roosevelt had each tried to return for a third and had failed. In 1940, Roosevelt broke the custom and ran for a third. Vice President Garner, who opposed a third term, was dropped, and Henry Wallace of Iowa, the Secretary of Agriculture, became the running mate. At the party convention in Chicago, a voice began booming over the loudspeakers that the people wanted Roosevelt, and the delegates took up the chant. His opponent, Wendell Willkie, was a business lawyer from Indiana who had been a Democrat until a year before. He supported aid to Britain, too, so the voters were not given a plain choice. Roosevelt won, with 449 electoral votes to 82. After his death, Americans wrote the two-term limit into the Constitution as the 22nd Amendment. During the campaign, he promised the mothers and fathers of America that their sons would not be sent into any foreign wars. That promise would soon be tested.

Should a long custom give way when a country is in danger? Decide first and jot down one reason on each side before you open this.
One side says a custom is only a custom, and a crisis may need an experienced leader. The other side says that the habit of leaving office protects a republic from a leader who stays too long. The lesson adds that his opponent, Wendell Willkie, also supported aid to Britain, so voters were not given a plain choice. A fair historian weighs both reasons and does not decide for you.

By late 1940, Britain was running out of money to pay for weapons. In December, Churchill wrote Roosevelt a long letter saying so plainly. Roosevelt read it while on a cruise in the Caribbean, and he came home with a new idea. In a radio address that month, he said that the country must become the great arsenal of democracy. He offered a simple picture. If your neighbor's house is on fire, he said, you do not argue about the price of your garden hose. You lend it, and you get it back when the fire is out. The result was the Lend-Leaselend LEESA 1941 law that let the president lend or lease weapons and supplies to any country whose defense he thought vital to the United States. Act, passed in March 1941. Its bill number was 1776. Senator Burton Wheeler, who had fought the court plan, said that it would plow under every fourth American boy. The Senate passed it by 60 votes to 31, and the House by 260 to 165. It allowed the president to lend or lease weapons and supplies to any country whose defense he thought was vital to the United States. By the end of the war, the United States had sent over fifty billion dollars of goods to its allies. The country called itself neutral, but it was no longer acting like a neutral.

Not everyone agreed, and isolationismeye-suh-LAY-shun-iz-umThe belief that a country should stay out of the quarrels and wars of other nations. was still strong. The America First Committeeuh-MAIR-ih-kuh furst kuh-MIT-eeA group, active in 1940 and 1941, that worked to keep the United States out of the war., founded in 1940, became the largest group fighting against American entry into the war. It had hundreds of thousands of members. It began among students at Yale, and one early member was a law student named Gerald Ford, who would one day be president. Its best-known speaker was the famous pilot Charles Lindbergh. Supporters of the group believed that America's defense depended on its own navy and air force, and that Britain could not be saved. They feared that Roosevelt was leading the country into war step by step. In a speech in Des Moines in September 1941, Lindbergh also blamed Jewish Americans and the British for pushing the country toward war, and that hurt the group's good name. Historians still disagree about whether the isolationists were wrong about the danger or simply wrong about the answer.

In June 1941, the war widened again when Hitler invaded the Soviet Union with about three million soldiers, breaking his deal with Stalin. In the fall, Roosevelt made the Soviet Union eligible for Lend-Lease aid. In August, Roosevelt and Churchill met on warships off Newfoundland. On the Sunday, sailors of both navies joined them on deck for a church service, and they sang hymns together. They issued the Atlantic Charterat-LAN-tik CHAR-terA statement of war aims issued by Roosevelt and Churchill in August 1941. It promised no new territory, self-government for peoples, and freedom of the seas., a joint statement of the goals they hoped to reach after the war. It promised that neither would seek new territory, that peoples should choose their own governments, and that the seas should be free. In July, American marines had taken over the defense of Iceland from the British, and by the fall the Navy was escorting convoys across much of the Atlantic. Meanwhile, German submarines and American ships began to fight. In September, after a German submarine fired torpedoes at the destroyer USS Greer, Roosevelt ordered the Navy to shoot German and Italian warships on sight in the waters America said it was defending. In October, a German submarine sank the destroyer USS Reuben James, which was escorting a convoy, killing about a hundred sailors. There was no declaration of war, but there was an undeclared naval conflict.

    In Asia, a different crisis was coming. Japan, which was already at war with China, joined Germany and Italy in 1940 in a pact. Together, the three became known as the Axis PowersAK-sis POW-urzGermany, Italy, and Japan, the nations that fought the Allies in World War II.. In the summer of 1941, Japan moved its troops into southern Indochina, which was then a French colony. Japan needed oil, rubber, and metal to keep fighting, and the colonies of Southeast Asia had them. Roosevelt answered with an embargoem-BAR-gohA government order that stops trade with another country., a government order that stops trade with another country. He froze Japanese money in American banks and cut off oil. Japan imported most of its oil from the United States, about four fifths of it. Japanese admirals calculated that their fleet's oil would run out in a year or two. The leaders in Tokyo now faced a choice. They could give up their conquests in China, or they could take the oil of Southeast Asia by force. They chose force, and they decided that to do so they must first destroy the American fleet. In October, General Hideki Tojo became prime minister of Japan. Talks in Washington went on through November. Secretary of State Cordell Hull told the Japanese that the embargo would end only if Japan pulled out of China and Indochina, and the Japanese leaders would not agree. American codebreakers had broken Japan's diplomatic code and knew that war was near, but the messages they read did not name the target. Japan's carrier fleet had already sailed for Hawaii by a northern route, in radio silence.

    On the morning of Sunday, December 7, 1941, Japanese planes launched from six aircraft carriers attacked the naval base at Pearl Harborpurl HAR-burThe American naval base in Hawaii that Japan attacked on December 7, 1941, bringing the United States into the war., Hawaii. Most of the fleet's battleships lay moored in a line beside Ford Island, an easy target for bombers. Minutes before the first bombs fell, two soldiers at a radar station on Oahu had seen a large group of planes on their screen. An officer told them not to worry, because American bombers were due from California that morning. The attack sank or badly damaged eight battleships and destroyed about 188 aircraft. The battleship Arizona blew up, and more than eleven hundred of her crew died. In all, the attack killed 2,403 Americans. The aircraft carriers of the Pacific Fleet were at sea that day, delivering planes to island bases, which later proved very important. The American commanders were surprised. They had known that war with Japan was likely, but they had expected an attack in Southeast Asia.

    The next day, Roosevelt asked Congress for a declaration of war. His speech lasted only a few minutes. He had dictated it himself, and in a famous change he crossed out the words "world history" and wrote "infamy" in their place. The Senate voted for it unanimously, and the House voted 388 to 1. The one vote against it came from Jeannette Rankin of Montana, the first woman ever elected to Congress, who had also voted against entering the Great War. Years of argument ended in a few hours. Three days later, Hitler declared war on the United States, though no treaty required him to do so. Italy did the same. It was one of the most important choices of the war. If he had not, Americans might have focused much of their effort on Japan alone. Instead, the United States was now at war with all three Axis powers.

    ⚡ HOW NEUTRAL?
    Before Pearl Harborpurl HAR-burThe American naval base in Hawaii that Japan attacked on December 7, 1941, bringing the United States into the war., the country still called itself neutral. How well did that word fit?

    Consider one more "what if." Suppose Japan had struck only British and Dutch colonies and left American territory alone. We cannot know what would have followed, but a country that had extended its draft by a single vote would have argued for months. Pearl Harbor left no room for that argument.

    Could America have stayed out? Some say no, because the Axis nations were planning to dominate their regions, and a free republic could not live in a world run by dictators. Others say the oil embargo pushed Japan into a corner, and that a more cautious policy might have kept the peace for a time. As you read the sources this week, notice what each president and each critic saw at the time, not what we know now.

The Unipolar Moment: War, Trade, and Boom

📖 TODAY'S LESSON

In 1991, the Cold War ended, and the United States was left as the strongest country in the world. No other nation could match its economy, its military, or its reach. A commentator named Charles Krauthammer called this a "unipolar moment." A unipolar worldyoo-nih-POH-ler WURLDA world with one great power, with no rival of equal strength. is one with a single great power, like a magnet with one pole. It is the opposite of the Cold War, when two superpowers each balanced the other. Some Americans hoped that the moment would last for a long time. Others warned that no country holds such a position forever. In this lesson, you will see how the United States used its power in the years after 1989, and what limits it met.

The first test came in the Middle East. In August 1990, Iraq's dictator, Saddam Husseinsah-DAHM hoo-SAYNThe dictator of Iraq from 1979 until 2003. His invasion of Kuwait led to the Persian Gulf War., sent his army into the small oil-rich country of Kuwait and took it in a few hours. The attack began before dawn on August 2, and Kuwait's ruling family fled across the border to Saudi Arabia. Saddam had just finished a long and costly war with Iran, and he owed large debts to Kuwait. He claimed that Kuwait had always belonged to Iraq, and he accused the Kuwaitis of pumping so much oil that prices fell, which hurt a country that earned nearly all of its income from oil. Now he controlled a large share of the world's oil, and his army threatened Saudi Arabia, the greatest oil producer of all. President George H. W. BushJORJ aych DUB-ul-yoo BUSHThe 41st president, from 1989 to 1993. He led the coalition in the Persian Gulf War. was a World War II navy pilot, a former head of the CIA, and an experienced diplomat. He said, "This will not stand."

Words alone would not stop tanks, and Saddam's army sat only a short drive from the Saudi oil fields. Bush met his advisers on the morning of the invasion, then flew to Colorado, where the British prime minister, Margaret Thatcher, was visiting. She urged him to stand firm. Within days, at the invitation of Saudi Arabia's King Fahd, American soldiers began landing in the Saudi desert, among them paratroopers of the 82nd Airborne Division. The operation was called Desert Shield, and its job was to defend, not to attack. At the same time, the United Nations ordered a trade embargo on Iraq, and warships stopped vessels that tried to carry Iraqi oil out or supplies in. By winter, more than half a million American troops were in the region, and every one of them needed food, water, fuel, and a place to sleep.

Bush set out to build a coalitionkoh-uh-LISH-unA group of nations or groups that join together for a common purpose., a group of nations joined for a common purpose. He worked through the United Nations Security Council, where the Soviet Union, in its last year, voted with the United States instead of using its veto. Secretary of State James Baker traveled the world to win support one leader at a time. In November, the Council set a deadline: Iraq must leave Kuwait by January 15, 1991, or the nations could use force to make it leave. More than thirty countries joined, including Britain, France, Egypt, and Saudi Arabia, and many shared the cost. In the end, the allies paid most of the bill. Bush also asked Congress for approval, and in January 1991, Congress voted to authorize the use of force. The vote was close, 52 to 47 in the Senate and 250 to 183 in the House, and many members had wanted to give the embargo more time to work. Nothing like this had been seen since Korea: a war fought under a UN resolution, with allies, and with Congress involved. In his address to Congress in September 1990, which you will read this week, Bush spoke of a "new world order," in which nations would work together to oppose aggression.

The Persian Gulf WarPER-zhun GULF WORThe 1991 war in which a coalition led by the United States drove Iraq's army out of Kuwait. began on January 17, 1991, with a huge air campaign. Cruise missiles and stealth planes hit Iraqi targets, and television audiences watched the first "live" war. Saddam answered by firing Scud missiles at Israel, a country that was not even in the war. He hoped Israel would strike back, because then the Arab members of the coalition might refuse to fight beside it. Bush asked the Israelis to hold their fire, and the United States rushed Patriot missile batteries to Israel to shoot down the Scuds. Israel, though under attack, did not retaliate. The coalition held together, and the air campaign went on for more than five weeks.

On February 24, the ground attack began. Iraq expected the blow to come from the south, into Kuwait's trenches and minefields, and Marines and Arab forces did attack there. The real weight of the coalition's army, though, swung far to the west through open desert and came around behind Iraq's best troops, the Republican Guard. The plan was the work of General Norman Schwarzkopf, who led the coalition's forces, and General Colin Powell, the chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff, who believed that if the United States went to war, it should use such overwhelming force that the fight would be short. In about a hundred hours, coalition forces drove Iraq's army out of Kuwait and destroyed a large part of it. American losses were light, fewer than 150 killed in combat, and Iraqi losses were many times higher. As Iraqi troops fled north out of Kuwait City, coalition aircraft struck the long columns of trucks and cars on the highway, and photographs of the burned vehicles disturbed many people.

Bush then stopped. On the evening of February 27, he announced that Kuwait was free, and the ceasefire began a few hours later. He did not march on Baghdad to remove Saddam, because he believed that this would break the coalition and leave America in charge of a hostile country. The United Nations had approved a war to free Kuwait, not to topple Iraq's government, and the Arab partners had joined for that reason. In a book written years later, Bush and his national security adviser, Brent Scowcroft, said that taking Baghdad would have meant ruling Iraq with no clear way out. Saddam stayed in power, and he crushed uprisings by Kurds and Shiites that Americans had encouraged. Bush had urged the Iraqi people to take matters into their own hands, and when they tried, Saddam's helicopters and tanks put the revolts down. Hundreds of thousands of Kurds fled into the mountains of the north, and the United States and its allies soon set up a protected zone there and barred Iraqi aircraft from flying over it. Critics said the war was left unfinished. Defenders said the goal, to free Kuwait, had been reached, and that Bush had avoided a trap. It is a good example of a leader deciding what to leave undone.

Despite the victory, Bush lost the election of 1992. At the end of the war, about nine in ten Americans approved of the job he was doing, one of the highest ratings ever recorded. It did not last. A recession had begun in 1990, and by the middle of 1992, unemployment had climbed to nearly eight percent. Bush also had trouble in his own party. In 1988, he had promised not to raise taxes, but in 1990, he agreed to a budget deal that raised some of them to shrink the deficit, and many Republicans never forgave him. Voters worried about jobs. A third-party candidate, the Texas businessman Ross Perot, took many votes. He paid for his own television ads, warned about the national debt, and won about nineteen percent of the vote. The winner was the governor of Arkansas, Bill ClintonBIL KLIN-tunThe 42nd president, from 1993 to 2001. He was a Democrat and the former governor of Arkansas., a young Democrat who promised to focus "like a laser" on the economy.

Clinton was a gifted speaker, and he had a quick mind for policy. His first budget raised taxes on high earners and cut spending to shrink the deficit. It passed in 1993 by the narrowest of margins, with the vice president casting the tie-breaking vote in the Senate, and not one Republican voted for it. In 1994, voters answered by giving Republicans control of both houses of Congress for the first time in forty years, and their leader in the House, Newt Gingrich, led them in a long fight with the president over the budget that shut down parts of the government twice in the winter of 1995 and 1996. Clinton also proved to be careful about the political center. He declared that "the era of big government is over," and in 1996 he signed a law that reformed welfare by setting time limits on benefits and requiring work. The law limited most families to five years of aid in a lifetime, and some officials in his own administration resigned in protest. He was reelected that year.

Clinton's most important economic decision was to follow Bush on free trade. The North American Free Trade Agreementnorth uh-MAIR-ih-kun FREE trayd uh-GREE-muntA treaty, called NAFTA, that removed most trade barriers among the United States, Canada, and Mexico. It took effect in 1994., or NAFTA, removed most trade barriers among the United States, Canada, and Mexico. Most of those barriers were tariffs, which are taxes on goods that cross a border, and NAFTA phased them out over a number of years. Bush had signed it in December 1992, but Congress had not yet voted. After a bitter fight in Congress, in which many labor unions and many members of Clinton's own party opposed it, Clinton won its passage in 1993, and it took effect on January 1, 1994. Perot warned that jobs would flow south across the border. Supporters said that free trade would lower prices and open new markets. Opponents said it would send factory jobs to Mexico. The truth is mixed. Trade grew sharply, and consumers gained from lower prices. But some towns that depended on manufacturing were hit hard, and the pain was real for the people who lived there. In 1995, the United States also helped create a new international body to set rules for trade among its members, and over the following years, trade with China grew rapidly.

At the same time, the economy took off. Much of the growth came from a new technology. The Internet had been a tool for scientists and the military. In the 1990s, it spread to homes, with the World Wide Web, web browsers, and online companies such as Amazon and Google. The first browser that ordinary people found easy to use appeared in 1993, and when Netscape, a company that sold a browser, offered its shares to the public in 1995, the price more than doubled on the first day. Amazon began in 1994 as an online bookstore started by Jeff Bezos. Google began in 1998, when two Stanford graduate students, Larry Page and Sergey Brin, built a better way to search the web. A company that lost money could still raise millions by selling shares, because investors feared missing the next Amazon. Investors poured money into any business with ".com" in its name. In 1996, the chairman of the Federal Reserve, Alan Greenspan, warned that investors seemed carried away by their own excitement, but the buying went on.

    The dot-com boomDOT-kom BOOMThe rush of investment in internet companies in the late 1990s that drove the stock market to new heights before many of the companies failed. pushed the stock market to new heights. The Nasdaq, an index heavy with technology stocks, passed 5,000 in March 2000. Unemployment fell to about four percent by 2000, and starting in 1998, for the first time in decades, the federal government took in more money than it spent. Rising paychecks and stock profits meant that more tax money was coming in. Not all of this was lasting. In 2000 and 2001, many internet companies failed, and stock prices fell. Pets.com, which sold pet supplies online and had advertised during the Super Bowl with a sock puppet, shut down within a year of selling its shares to the public. By 2002, the Nasdaq had lost about three fourths of its value. An old lesson was learned again: speculation can run ahead of reality.

    The decade was a test of how America would use its power without a rival. In some places, it acted. When Yugoslavia broke apart, war came to Bosnia in 1992, and about a hundred thousand people died. In July 1995, Serb forces killed roughly eight thousand Muslim men and boys at Srebrenica. NATO then bombed Serb positions, and talks at an air force base in Dayton, Ohio, produced a peace agreement that November, with American soldiers sent as part of a NATO force to keep the peace. In this way, the United States helped end the war in Bosnia in 1995. In 1999, Serbian forces under Slobodan Milosevic drove thousands of ethnic Albanians from their homes in Kosovo, and the United States and its allies bombed Serbian forces for 78 days until he gave way. No American was killed in combat.

    In others, it held back or failed. In late 1992, Bush had sent troops to Somalia to guard food shipments for people starving in a civil war, but under Clinton the mission grew into a hunt for a warlord named Mohamed Farrah Aidid. In 1993, eighteen American soldiers died in Somalia in a street battle in Mogadishu, and television showed the body of one of them dragged through the streets. Clinton pulled out. In 1994, in Rwanda, about eight hundred thousand people were killed in a genocide, and the world did not act. The killing lasted about a hundred days, and most of the dead were Tutsi, murdered by Hutu extremists, often their own neighbors. A small United Nations force already on the ground was reduced instead of strengthened. Clinton said years later that the world, including the United States, had not acted quickly enough. American leaders drew a hard lesson from Somalia and were reluctant to send troops to places where no vital interest seemed to be at stake. They also wondered what role a country with no rival should play.

The League Rejected, 1919 to 1922

📖 TODAY'S LESSON

On November 11, 1918, the guns of the Great War fell silent. The ArmisticeAR-mih-stisAn agreement to stop fighting. The Armistice of November 11, 1918, ended the Great War's fighting but did not settle the peace., signed in a railway car in a French forest, stopped the fighting, but it did not write the peace. That job fell to President Woodrow Wilson, and he did something no sitting president had ever done. He sailed to Europe to join the peace talks in person, landing in France in mid-December. Crowds in Paris, London, and Rome cheered him as a hero. Wilson carried a plan, the Fourteen PointsFOR-teen pointsPresident Wilson's January 1918 plan for peace. The last point called for a league of nations., which he had announced to Congress in January 1918. The last point called for a "general association of nations" to keep the peace. That idea became the League of Nationsleeg uv NAY-shunzThe international organization created by the Treaty of Versailles to keep the peace. The United States never joined it..

The peace conference opened in Paris in January 1919, and the real decisions were made by four men: Wilson; David Lloyd George, the prime minister of Britain; Vittorio Orlando of Italy; and Georges Clemenceau, the prime minister of France. They met behind closed doors, day after day, while the smaller nations waited outside. Clemenceau was nicknamed the Tiger, and he had his reasons for being hard. In his lifetime, German armies had marched into France twice, and he meant to make sure they never did so again. Wilson's country lay across an ocean and had seen no fighting on its own soil. That made it easier for him to speak of a peace for the whole world.

The League was meant to work like a promise among neighbors. Its Covenant, or founding document, asked members to settle quarrels by talking, to wait before going to war, and to act together against any nation that attacked another. A member that went to war in defiance of the rules faced a cutoff of trade with every other member. Every member would have a seat in an Assembly, and the great powers would sit on a smaller Council. Its home would be Geneva, in Switzerland. Wilson did not leave the writing to others. He chaired the commission that drafted the Covenant, and he insisted that it be built into the peace treaty itself. He cared about it more than about any other part of the peace.

Wilson was a former college president and a scholar of government. He was brilliant and sincere, and he also had a stubborn streak. He disliked giving ground once he believed he was right. At Paris, he won the League, but he gave up much else to get it. Britain and France wanted to punish Germany and make it pay. The Treaty of VersaillesTREE-tee uv ver-SYThe 1919 treaty that ended the Great War with Germany. It blamed Germany for the war and created the League of Nations., signed on June 28, 1919, blamed Germany for the war and charged it heavy payments. The place and the date were chosen with care. The signing took place in the Hall of Mirrors at the palace of Versailles, the same room where, in 1871, a victorious Germany had proclaimed its empire after beating France. June 28 was also five years to the day since the murder of Archduke Franz Ferdinand, the killing that had set off the war. Wilson tied the League to the treaty itself, so the Senate could not approve one without the other. It was a choice that would be tested in a different building, an ocean away.

Under the Constitution, a treaty needs the approval of two-thirds of the Senate. To ratifyRAT-ih-fyTo give official approval. The Senate must approve a treaty by a two-thirds vote to ratify it. a treaty means to give that approval. The Senate had ninety-six members, so if all of them voted, a treaty needed sixty-four yes votes. Wilson, a Democrat, faced a Senate where Republicans held a narrow majority after the 1918 elections, forty-nine seats to forty-seven. The man who led the Republican side was Senator Henry Cabot LodgeHEN-ree KAB-ut lojThe Massachusetts senator who led Senate opposition to the Treaty of Versailles without changes. He chaired the Foreign Relations Committee. of Massachusetts, chairman of the Foreign Relations Committee. Lodge was a historian, a writer, and a hard political fighter. He and Wilson disliked each other deeply, and the feeling was old. When the treaty reached the Senate in July, Lodge used his power as chairman to slow everything down. He had the whole text read aloud to his committee, hundreds of pages of it, which took about two weeks, and then he held hearings for weeks more. Every day of delay gave the opposition more time to organize.

The Senate split three ways. About a dozen senators, called irreconcilablesih-REK-un-sy-luh-bulzThe senators who opposed joining the League of Nations on any terms., opposed the League on any terms. Two of the best known were William Borah of Idaho and Hiram Johnson of California, both famous speakers who carried their case to crowds across the country. Most Democrats stood with Wilson. In the middle were senators who would accept the League if it came with changes. Lodge led this group. He drew up a list of reservationsrez-er-VAY-shunzChanges or conditions that the Senate attaches to a treaty before it will approve it., which are changes or conditions attached to a treaty. There were fourteen of them, a number many people took as a jab at Wilson's Fourteen Points. The most important one dealt with Article 10AR-tih-kul tenThe part of the League of Nations Covenant that promised members would protect one another against attack. Senator Lodge feared it could pull the United States into war without a vote of Congress. of the Covenant. Article 10 promised that members would protect one another from attack, an idea called collective securitykuh-LEK-tiv sih-KYOOR-ih-teeThe idea that nations should protect one another from attack by promising to act together against an aggressor.. Lodge feared that this promise could drag the nation into a foreign war without a vote of Congress. Under the Constitution, only Congress can declare war. For Lodge, this was no small point. It went to the heart of who rules in a republic. The arithmetic mattered too. A reservation needed only a simple majority, while the treaty itself still needed two-thirds. Wilson's Democrats could not reach sixty-four votes alone. To win, he needed at least some of Lodge's group.

In August, Wilson tried to answer the doubts face to face. He invited the Foreign Relations Committee to the White House and spent hours answering questions. The hardest question was about Article 10. If a member were attacked, would the United States have to send its army? Wilson said that the League could only advise, and that the nation would be bound by its honor, a moral duty and not a legal one. To Lodge and his allies, that answer did not calm the worry. It sharpened it. A promise of honor, made without any vote by Congress, was exactly the danger they had named.

Wilson believed the American people stood with him, and in September 1919 he set out to prove it. He took a speaking tour of the West, riding more than eight thousand miles by train. There was no radio to carry his voice across the country, so he had to go to the people, city by city, beginning in Columbus, Ohio. He spoke almost every day, sometimes twice, in auditoriums and ballparks and from the back platform of his railroad car. He was already worn out from the long months in Paris, and he suffered from terrible headaches. On September 25, after a speech in Pueblo, Colorado, he broke down and the tour ended. The Pueblo speech was one of his best, and he came close to tears at the end as he spoke of American soldiers buried in France. That night, on the train, he could not sleep. In the morning he told his secretary that he had gone to pieces. The rest of the tour was canceled, and the train turned back toward Washington.

A week later, on October 2, in the White House, he suffered a severe stroke that paralyzed his left side. His wife, Edith, found him on the floor of his bathroom. For months, few outsiders saw him, and the public was told only that he was exhausted. His wife and his doctor controlled who could. Senators who wanted to know his wishes about the treaty had to learn them secondhand. The president was never again fully able to lead the fight.

The Senate voted on November 19, 1919. First came the treaty with Lodge's reservations. Wilson urged his Democratic allies to vote no, sending word through the Democratic leader, Senator Gilbert Hitchcock of Nebraska, because he thought the changes would weaken the League, and the treaty failed, 39 votes to 55. The no votes came from two groups that agreed on nothing else: Wilson's loyal Democrats, who wanted the treaty without changes, and the irreconcilables, who wanted no treaty at all. Next came the treaty with no changes. Lodge's followers and the irreconcilables voted no, and it failed too, by 38 to 53. A third try in March 1920 also fell short of two-thirds.

The third try came closest. Through the winter, senators from both parties met to look for a compromise, and Wilson would not give them one. In March, the treaty with Lodge's changes won 49 votes to 35, seven short of what it needed. About twenty Democrats had by then broken with the president and voted for it. In November, they had followed him. By March, they had decided that a League with changes was better than none.

Many historians think the League could have passed if Wilson had accepted a deal. But nobody can be sure. Perhaps Lodge would have kept adding demands. Perhaps a healthy Wilson would have bargained, as he had in Paris. Perhaps a sick Wilson simply could not bend. The outcome depended on stubborn men, bad health, and old grudges. It was not written in advance, and a few changed choices might have changed it.

The voters had their say in 1920. Wilson had asked that the election be treated as a solemn referendum on the League. Republican Warren G. HardingWOR-en jee HAR-dingThe Republican who won the 1920 presidential election by promising a return to normalcy. He died in office in 1923., a senator from Ohio and a small-town newspaper publisher, promised a "return to normalcyNOR-mul-seeWarren Harding's word for a calm return to ordinary life after the war and years of reform.," a calmer life after years of war and reform. He beat Democrat James M. Cox in a landslide, taking more than sixty percent of the vote and 404 electoral votes to Cox's 127. Cox's running mate, a young New Yorker named Franklin D. Roosevelt, had campaigned for the League. The League had become tied to Wilson's name, and many voters were tired of crusades. Voters had many reasons to choose Harding, and Harding's own words about the League were vague, so nobody can say how many meant their ballots as a vote against it. The United States never joined the League of Nations.

Staying out of the League did not mean hiding from the world. In July 1921, Congress ended the state of war with Germany, and Harding signed the resolution. That same year, Secretary of State Charles Evans Hughes invited the great naval powers to the Washington Naval ConferenceWASH-ing-tun NAY-vul KON-fer-unsThe 1921 to 1922 meeting where the great naval powers agreed to limit the size of their battleship fleets.. Hughes opened it with a stunning offer. He did not speak in general terms. He named ships, one by one, that his own country was ready to give up. The United States would scrap ships it was building if Britain and Japan did the same. The treaty that followed set a ratio for the largest warships. For every five tons of battleships Britain or the United States kept, Japan could keep three, and France and Italy were given smaller shares. The Senate approved it in 1922. It was a major agreement made outside the League. The Republic would work with other nations, but on its own terms, treaty by treaty, with the Senate watching.

Two other treaties came out of the same conference. In one, the United States, Britain, France, and Japan promised to respect one another's island possessions in the Pacific. In the other, nine nations promised to respect China's independence and to keep its markets open to all. Notice what these agreements did not contain. None of them promised that the United States would fight for anyone. That was the kind of promise Lodge had feared in Article 10, and it was missing here.

Was this a wise choice? Defenders of Lodge said a republic must keep the power over war and peace in the hands of its own elected representatives. Critics said America had helped win the war and then walked away from the peace, leaving Britain and France to carry a burden they could not carry alone. Both arguments have force. A nation's first duty is its own defense, and defense depends on a government that can deliberate and act. But a world without American weight in its councils proved less stable than Wilson had hoped. The price of that choice would not come due for another twenty years.

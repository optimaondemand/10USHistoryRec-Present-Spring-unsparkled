📖 TODAY'S LESSON

In March 1985, the Soviet Politburo chose a new leader. Mikhail Gorbachevmih-KYLE GOR-buh-chofThe last leader of the Soviet Union, from 1985 to 1991. He tried to reform the system, and the reforms helped bring it down. was fifty-four, young for a Soviet ruler, and he was the first Soviet leader born after the revolution of 1917. He had seen Stalin's terror at close range as a boy, for both of his grandfathers had been arrested. He grew up on a farm in southern Russia and studied law in Moscow before he climbed through the Communist Party. He knew that the system was in trouble. Stores were empty, and factories made goods that no one wanted. In the Soviet system, planners in Moscow, not customers, decided how many shoes or tractors a factory should make. Factories were rewarded for meeting their quotas, whether or not anyone wanted the goods, so shoes piled up in warehouses while shoppers stood in long lines for the ones they needed. The army, meanwhile, was costing a huge share of the national income. Oil prices had fallen, and the Soviet Union depended on oil for much of the money it earned from other countries. Gorbachev wanted to save the system, not to end it. He believed that communism could be reformed, and he began with two ideas.

The first was glasnostGLAHZ-nohstA Russian word meaning "openness." It was Gorbachev's policy of letting Soviet citizens discuss and criticize their government., a Russian word meaning "openness." For the first time, Soviet newspapers and writers could criticize the government, and ordinary people could discuss the country's past. The old habit of secrecy was tested in April 1986, when a reactor exploded at the Chernobyl nuclear plant in Ukraine. For days the government said almost nothing, while a cloud of radiation drifted across Europe. Gorbachev later said that the disaster showed him how costly secrecy could be. He also freed Andrei Sakharov, a famous physicist and critic of the government, from exile in the city of Gorky. The second idea was perestroikapair-uh-STROY-kuhA Russian word meaning "restructuring." It was Gorbachev's plan to change the Soviet economy and allow some private business., meaning "restructuring." It allowed some private business and gave factory managers more freedom. Families could now open small cooperatives, such as restaurants and repair shops. At first the changes made shortages worse, because the old planning no longer worked and the new market had not yet taken its place. In 1989, voters chose a new national legislature in the first partly free elections that the Soviet Union had held since 1917. Both ideas had a surprising effect. Once people were allowed to talk, they began to say aloud what they had long believed, and the party lost much of its power to frighten them. A reform meant to repair the machine instead began to shake it apart.

Gorbachev also wanted to ease the cost of the arms race. He and Reagan met for the first time in Geneva in 1985, in a lakeside house, and they talked for hours. A year later, in Reykjavik, Iceland, they met again in a small house by the sea, with only a few aides and interpreters. Over two days the talks went further than anyone had expected. They came close to agreeing to remove all nuclear weapons. On the last day, though, Gorbachev said that the deal depended on keeping missile-defense testing inside the laboratory. Reagan said no, and the talks broke down over Reagan's refusal to give up the Strategic Defense Initiative. Some of Reagan's advisers had been alarmed at how far he was willing to go, and so were some of America's allies. Both men left the house in a grim mood. Even so, the two men had built trust.

Another is that the deal would have fallen apart under pressure at home. What the lesson does show is that the two men left the house with trust, even though the meeting itself failed. Small turns in a meeting can change a story, but they do not always change where it ends.

In December 1987, they signed the Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treatyin-ter-MEE-dee-it RAYNJ NOO-klee-er FOR-siz TREE-teeA 1987 treaty in which the United States and the Soviet Union agreed to destroy a whole class of nuclear missiles. It also allowed inspections. in Washington. It was the first agreement to destroy a whole class of nuclear weapons, rather than just limit their growth. The class was land-based missiles that could fly between about three hundred and three thousand four hundred miles, and by 1991 about 2,700 of them had been destroyed. The treaty also allowed each side to inspect the other's sites, and inspectors from each country watched the other's missile factories. During the Washington visit, Gorbachev stopped his car in the middle of a street to shake hands with the crowds. Reagan used an old Russian proverb at the signing: "Trust, but verify." The Senate approved the treaty in 1988 by a wide margin.

Reagan's own position on the Soviets had changed too. The man who had called them an evil empire now said that he did not think of Gorbachev that way. In Red Square in 1988, a reporter asked whether he still thought of the Soviet Union as an evil empire. Reagan said that he had been talking about another time and another era. Some historians argue that Reagan's firm stance, followed by his willingness to deal, got the best result. Others stress that Gorbachev was the one who made the choices, and that a different Soviet leader might have used force to keep the empire together. Both views are probably partly right, and this is where contingency matters. In June 1987, Reagan stood before the Brandenburg Gate in West Berlin, within sight of the wall that divided the city, and called on Gorbachev to "tear down this wall." You will read the address this week. Some of his own advisers had urged him to cut the line, fearing that it would sound too harsh, but Reagan kept it. At the time, it seemed to many like a hopeless demand.

The wall fell sooner than anyone expected. Reagan had left office in January 1989, before it happened. The Soviet Union's allies in Eastern Europe had been held in place for forty years by Soviet tanks. Gorbachev announced that he would no longer use them. He said so plainly in a speech to the United Nations in December 1988, where he also promised to cut the Soviet army by half a million men and said that every nation had the right to choose its own path. In Poland, a free labor union called Solidaritysol-ih-DAIR-ih-teeA free labor union in Poland, led by Lech Walesa, that grew into a movement that helped end communist rule there., led by an electrician named Lech Walesa, a winner of the Nobel Peace Prize, and backed by the Catholic Church and Pope John Paul II, forced the government to hold elections. The union had begun in 1980 in the shipyards of Gdansk, and the government had cracked down on it in 1981, but it had never really disappeared. Waves of strikes over rising prices in 1988 showed the government that it could no longer rule alone. In 1989 the Polish government, short of money and out of ideas, sat down at a round table with its opponents and agreed to a vote. In June 1989, Solidarity won nearly every seat it was allowed to contest. In the summer, Hungary, which had already begun taking down the barbed-wire fence on its border with Austria, opened that border, and thousands of East Germans fled to the West.

⚡ HOW MUCH DID ONE DECISION MATTER?
Soviet tanks had held the governments of Eastern Europe in place for forty years. Gorbachev told them the tanks would stay home. How much did that decision matter?

Huge crowds gathered in East German cities to demand freedom. The biggest were in Leipzig, where people marched every Monday evening after services in the churches. On October 9, about seventy thousand people marched, chanting that they were the people. The police and soldiers were ready, but in the end they held their fire. Only a few months before, the Chinese army had crushed student protests in Tiananmen Square in Beijing, so everyone knew that a government could still choose force. Nine days after the march in Leipzig, East Germany's hard-line leader, Erich Honecker, was forced out. The government that had built the Berlin Wall could not stop them.

The Berlin Wall had gone up in 1961 to stop East Germans from fleeing to the West, and it had stood for twenty-eight years. At least a hundred people had died trying to cross it. On the night of November 9, 1989, an East German official, Guenter Schabowski, speaking at a news conference, said by mistake that new travel rules took effect "immediately." He had been handed a note about the rules only a short time before, and they were meant to begin the next day, with permits. Thousands of people rushed to the wall. At one crossing, an officer named Harald Jaeger called his superiors again and again and got no clear order. As the crowd pressed against the gate, he finally let people through. The guards, unsure what to do, did not shoot, and the crossings were opened. In the first few days, hundreds of thousands of East Germans crossed to see the West. People climbed on the wall and swung hammers at it. The sight was shown on televisions around the world. Within weeks, Czechoslovakia's communists gave up power in what became known as the Velvet RevolutionVEL-vit rev-uh-LOO-shunThe peaceful end of communist rule in Czechoslovakia in late 1989. It was called "velvet" because there was almost no violence., so called because it came almost without bloodshed. Its leader was a playwright named Vaclav Havel, who had spent years in prison for criticizing the government, and by the end of December he was the president. Romania's revolution that December was violent. In Bucharest, the dictator Nicolae Ceausescu tried to speak to a crowd and was shouted down. He fled, was captured, and was executed on Christmas Day after a short trial. By the end of the year, communist rule in Eastern Europe was gone. Germany was officially reunited in October 1990, with the support of the United States. Gorbachev agreed that the united Germany could stay in the Western alliance, NATO.

The collapse spread to the Soviet Union itself. Its fifteen republics, including Ukraine, Lithuania, and Georgia, began to demand independence. Lithuania declared itself independent in March 1990, and in January 1991 Soviet troops killed fourteen people at the television tower in its capital, Vilnius. In August 1991, communist hard-liners tried to take power in a coup. The plotters, who included the head of the secret police and the defense minister, moved while Gorbachev was on vacation in Crimea, and they cut his phone lines. They placed Gorbachev under house arrest, but ordinary people in Moscow, led by Boris Yeltsin, the leader of the Russian republic, whom Russian voters had elected that summer, stood in front of the tanks. Yeltsin climbed onto a tank outside the Russian parliament building and called on the people to resist, and some of the soldiers sent against them refused to fire. The coup failed within three days. Gorbachev flew home to Moscow, but he found that real power had slipped away from him to Yeltsin and the leaders of the republics. The coup destroyed what was left of the party's authority. On December 1, Ukrainians voted overwhelmingly for independence, and a week later the leaders of Russia, Ukraine, and Belarus announced that the Soviet Union no longer existed. On December 25, 1991, Gorbachev resigned. That evening the red flag was lowered from the Kremlin, and the next day the Soviet legislature voted the Soviet Union out of existence. Fifteen new nations took its place. The Cold War was over, without a nuclear exchange, and the United States stood as the world's only superpower.

Put these events in order. Tap them one at a time.

    Why did it end? Historians give several answers. Some point to America's strength: its military buildup, its economic growth, and its alliances. Others point to the Soviet system itself, which could not meet the needs of its people. Some credit leaders such as Reagan, Gorbachev, and Pope John Paul II. Others give the credit to ordinary people in Poland, East Germany, and elsewhere who risked their lives. In truth, many causes worked together. The cause that was missing is the one that people feared most. Nuclear war did not come. A classical republican might note that the Western alliance had kept up its guard for four decades, and that its patience and the habits of free societies proved stronger than the discipline of a closed one.

    There is a lesson about humility too. Almost no one predicted the end of the Soviet Union, even in 1985, and certainly not in the form it took. Gorbachev, who hoped to save it, helped to end it. Reagan, who is often praised for a plan, could not have known how events would turn out. Contingency means that history can surprise even the experts. In China, the Communist Party used force in 1989 and kept power, a reminder that the Soviet ending was not the only one that was possible. In this week's discussion, you will decide how much of the credit each person and factor deserves, and who, in the end, "won."

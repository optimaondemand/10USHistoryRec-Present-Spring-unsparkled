📖 TODAY'S LESSON

By 1932, about one American worker in four had no job. Economists measure this with the unemployment rateun-em-PLOY-ment raytThe share of people who want to work but cannot find a job., the share of people who want to work but cannot find a job. The country had no unemployment insurance and no federal system of relief. Wisconsin passed the first state law for unemployment insurance that same year, but it did not pay anything right away. A worker who lost a job often lost the family's income at once. Savings had often disappeared in the bank runsbangk runA sudden rush by many depositors to withdraw their money because they fear their bank will fail. Banks lend out most of their deposits, so a run can ruin even a healthy bank. you read about last time. Families fell behind on their mortgage payments, and banks took their homes in a foreclosurefor-KLOH-zhurA bank's taking of a home or farm when the owner cannot make the mortgage payments.. Farm families lost their land the same way. In 1932 and 1933, farmers in several states crowded into the sales of foreclosed farms and scared off other bidders. Neighbors would bid a few pennies, and the farm would be sold back to the family for almost nothing.

Daily life changed in many small ways. Families doubled up, with grandparents, cousins, and grown children crowding under one roof. Couples put off marriage, and the birth rate fell. Schools suffered too. Some districts shortened the school year, and some rural schools closed early, because the money had run out. Many teenagers left school to work or left home to ride freight trains in search of jobs, and there were hundreds of thousands of them. Some families sent a son away because it meant one less mouth to feed. The young riders slept in railyards and begged for food at back doors, and the trains were dangerous. Many wives took in laundry or sewing to bring in a little money, and more married women looked for jobs. A law passed in 1932 said that if both a husband and a wife worked for the federal government, one of them had to resign, and most of the people who lost their jobs were women.

Those who lost their homes built shacks out of crates and scrap metal on the edges of cities. These shantytowns were bitterly named HoovervillesHOO-ver-vilA shantytown built by homeless people during the Great Depression and bitterly named after the president., after the president. The one in Seattle lasted for nine years. Men turned their empty pockets inside out and called them Hoover flags. A car pulled by a horse or mule, because its owner could no longer afford gasoline, was called a Hoover cart, and the stew ladled out at soup kitchens was sometimes called Hoover stew. Men who slept outdoors pulled newspapers over themselves for warmth and called them Hoover blankets. In the cities, hungry people stood in a breadlineBRED-lynA line of hungry people waiting for free food from a charity or church. outside churches and charities waiting for free food. On city streets, men in once-good suits sold apples for five cents apiece to earn a few coins. Many felt deep shame. They had always worked hard, and now they could not feed their families.

It is also true that three out of four workers still had jobs, even in the worst year. For those who kept working, falling prices made the paycheck go further. Prices fell by nearly a quarter between 1929 and 1933. The Great Depressiongrayt dih-PRESH-unThe worst economic crisis in American history. It began with the 1929 crash and lasted through the 1930s. was a catastrophe for millions, but it was not the same catastrophe for everyone.

People also found ways to lift their spirits. Movie theaters stayed busy, because a dime bought a few hours of escape. Some gave away a dish with every ticket to bring in customers on slow nights. Families gathered around the radio for comedy, music, and baseball. In 1935, a board game called Monopoly, in which players buy property and grow rich, became a surprise hit. Historians often note the irony of a nation of poor families playing at wealth. It suggests that Americans held on to hope even when their daily lives gave them little reason.

Some groups were hit much harder. Black Americans were often "last hired, first fired." In some northern cities, Black unemployment ran twice as high as white unemployment, or worse. In the South, Black sharecroppers and farm workers had been poor before the crash, and now they had almost nothing. Local relief offices often helped white families first. In some places, Black families were given smaller amounts than white families, or were turned away. Justice could be harsh as well. In 1931, nine Black teenagers riding a freight train through Alabama were accused of attacking two white women. Within weeks, an all-white jury had sentenced eight of them to death. The case, known by the name of the town of Scottsboro, drew attention across the country and around the world, and the Supreme Court later ordered new trials because the young men had not had proper lawyers.

Mexican Americans in the Southwest and elsewhere faced a different pressure. Officials in many cities and counties pushed families to leave the country, a policy called repatriationree-pay-tree-AY-shunSending people back to their country of origin. In the Depression, hundreds of thousands of people of Mexican descent, many of them American citizens, were pushed to leave.. Hundreds of thousands of people went to Mexico, some under pressure and some by force, and many of them were American citizens who had been born in the United States. In Los Angeles in 1931, agents raided a public plaza where Mexican families gathered. Local officials found that chartering a train to Mexico cost less than keeping a family on relief, and trains left carrying hundreds of people at a time.

On the Great Plains, nature added a second disaster. Farm prices had been falling since the 1920s. Then, beginning in 1931, a severe drought struck. In earlier years, farmers had plowed up the tough prairie grass to plant wheat, encouraged by high wartime prices and by the new tractor, which let a family farm far more land than horses could. Now nothing held the soil in place. Winds lifted the dry dirt into huge clouds, some of them miles high, that turned noon into night. One of the worst, on April 14, 1935, was remembered as Black Sunday. The region came to be called the Dust Bowldust bohlThe region of the Great Plains hit by drought and giant dust storms in the 1930s. Many farm families left it.. Crops died, animals choked, and families gave up. Hundreds of thousands left the Plains, and many headed west to California in old trucks piled high with their belongings. Many followed U.S. Route 66, the highway that ran from Chicago across the Plains to Los Angeles. Most came from Oklahoma, Texas, Arkansas, and Missouri, but Californians called them all "Okies," a name that was often meant as an insult. In April 1935, a soil expert named Hugh Bennett was testifying to a committee of Congress about the danger when dust from the Plains reached Washington and dimmed the sky. He is said to have pointed toward the window and told the senators that this was what he had been talking about. Congress soon passed a soil conservation law.

What did Americans do when the money ran out? The republican tradition says that a healthy civic culture produces cooperation without orders from above, and this happened across the country. Neighbors shared food. Churches ran soup kitchens. Lodges, unions, and local charities collected money. People traded work for meals. Some towns printed their own paper money, called scrip, to keep local trade going. President HooverHER-bert HOO-verThe mining engineer and relief organizer who was elected president in 1928. He led the nation as the Great Depression began. had counted on exactly this kind of voluntary help, and for a time it worked. But the scale of the disaster was too great. Cities depended on property taxes, and when homeowners could not pay, the cities lost the money they needed to help the poor. Cities went broke, and some could not pay their teachers. In Chicago, teachers went months without pay. Private charity ran dry. In 1931, New York's governor, Franklin D. RooseveltFRANK-lin dee ROH-zuh-veltThe governor of New York who was elected president in 1932 and served until 1945., set up a state relief program with state money, run by a social worker named Harry Hopkins, who would soon matter much more, and some other states followed. Reliefrih-LEEFMoney or goods given directly to people in need., in this sense, means money or goods given directly to people in need. Local relief usually came with strings. An investigator visited the home to ask what the family owned and who in it could work, and help often arrived as an order for groceries instead of cash. A family had to show that it had almost nothing left before it received anything. The question was whether the national government should provide it.

⚡ HOW WELL DID IT HOLD UP?
HooverHER-bert HOO-verThe mining engineer and relief organizer who was elected president in 1928. He led the nation as the Great Depression began. counted on voluntary help from families, churches, and charities. How well did that hold up as hard times went on?

Anger was rising even before that summer. In March 1932, thousands of jobless workers marched to the Ford plant in Dearborn, Michigan, to ask for work. Guards and police fired on the crowd, and at least four marchers were killed. The sharpest test came in the summer of 1932. In 1924, Congress had promised veterans of the Great War a bonus, but not to be paid until 1945. In 1932, that meant waiting thirteen more years. The promise took the form of a certificate worth about a dollar for each day served at home and a dollar and a quarter for each day overseas. With no jobs, many veterans wanted the money right away. In the spring and summer, thousands of them marched to Washington. The march began in Portland, Oregon, in May, led by a former sergeant named Walter Waters, and men joined along the way, hopping freight trains and hitching rides. Together with their families, perhaps twenty thousand or more camped in shacks and tents, many of them across the Anacostia River from the Capitol. They called themselves the Bonus ArmyBOH-nus AR-meeThousands of Great War veterans who camped in Washington in 1932 to demand early payment of a promised bonus. Army troops drove them out in July.. Washington's police chief, Pelham Glassford, himself a former general, treated them kindly and helped find food and tents. The camp had streets and rules and a daily routine, and the men organized themselves by state.

The House passed a bill to pay the bonus early, but the Senate voted it down on June 17. Thousands of veterans waited on the Capitol grounds for the news. When they heard, they did not riot. They sang "America" and walked back to their camps. Many veterans then went home, but thousands stayed.

On July 28, police tried to clear some buildings that the veterans occupied. Shots were fired, and two veterans were killed. Hoover then ordered the Army to clear the area. General Douglas MacArthurDUG-lus muk-AR-thurThe Army general who led the troops that drove the Bonus Army out of Washington in 1932. led the troops, which included infantry, cavalry with drawn sabers, and tanks. Major George Patton led the cavalry, and MacArthur's aide, Major Dwight Eisenhower, thought the Army's chief of staff should not be there at all. They used tear gas and drove the veterans across the river. MacArthur went farther than the president had ordered. He crossed the bridge and cleared the main camp, which went up in flames, even though Hoover had told the troops to stay on the near side. MacArthur told reporters that the marchers were a mob close to revolution. Americans watched the scene in newsreels. Hoover claimed that many of the veterans were Communists and criminals, but investigators found that most were genuine veterans. The country was shocked at the sight of soldiers turning on men who had once worn the same uniform.

    Whatever Hoover intended, the damage was done. It raised a hard question about the Republic. Who commands the Army, and how far may it go in dealing with citizens who are protesting? The bonus itself was not forgotten. In 1936, Congress passed a bill to pay it early, and it did so over President Roosevelt's veto. You will read Hoover's own explanation of these events in the next page.

    That November, voters gave their answer to the larger question. Roosevelt, the Democratic governor of New York, defeated Hoover in a landslide. In July, he had broken with custom by flying to the party's convention in Chicago to accept the nomination in person, instead of waiting at home to be told. He carried forty-two of the forty-eight states, and he won 472 electoral votes to Hoover's 59. But the new president would not take office until March 1933. In the long wait, the banks kept failing and the country sank deeper. Hoover asked Roosevelt to join him in a common plan, but Roosevelt did not want to be tied to Hoover's policies, and he refused. The 20th Amendment, ratified in January 1933, would shorten that wait for future presidents, but it did not apply to this one. The story of what Roosevelt did when he arrived is the subject of the next week.

📖 TODAY'S LESSON

Japan's attack on Pearl Harbor was only one part of a larger plan. In the same weeks, Japanese forces struck the Philippines, Malaya, Hong Kong, and the Dutch East Indies. They won victory after victory. On Wake Island, a small force of Marines held out for two weeks before it was overrun. Hours after Pearl Harbor, Japanese bombers caught American planes on the ground in the Philippines and destroyed many of them. In February 1942, the British fortress of Singapore fell, with some eighty thousand soldiers, in the largest surrender in British history. By the spring of 1942, Japan ruled a huge empire that stretched from the borders of India to islands near Australia. One bright spot came in April. Sixteen Army bombers led by Lieutenant Colonel James Doolittle took off from the deck of the Navy ship Hornet and bombed Tokyo. The raid did little damage, and none of the planes could return to the ship, so their crews bailed out or crash-landed in China. But it showed Americans that Japan could be struck, and it showed Japan's leaders that their home islands were not safe. Doolittle expected to be court-martialed for losing his planes, and was given the Medal of Honor instead. Americans had agreed on a policy of Europe FirstYUR-up furstThe Allied plan to defeat Germany before Japan., so the forces in the Pacific had to make do with less. They would need skill, courage, and some luck to survive.

The first great defeat came in the Philippines. General Douglas MacArthur commanded the American and Filipino troops there. In March 1942, he was ordered to leave for Australia, and he slipped away at night in a small torpedo boat. He told the Filipinos, "I shall return." On the Bataan Peninsula, about 75,000 American and Filipino soldiers, sick and starving, surrendered in April. The island fortress of Corregidor, under General Jonathan Wainwright, held out for another month. Most of the prisoners from Bataan were Filipinos. The Japanese forced them to march about sixty-five miles to a prison camp, in what is known as the Bataan Death Marchbuh-TAN deth marchThe forced march, in April 1942, of about 75,000 American and Filipino prisoners in the Philippines. Thousands died.. In the tropical heat, with little water, thousands died from beatings, hunger, and disease, or were killed when they could not go on. Americans learned of it in 1944, and it hardened the nation's will to fight.

The war turned in June 1942 at the Battle of MidwayBAT-ul uv MID-wayThe June 1942 naval battle in which American carrier planes sank four Japanese carriers and turned the tide in the Pacific.. The Pacific Fleet was led by Admiral Chester NimitzCHES-ter NIM-itsThe admiral who commanded the American Pacific Fleet., a Texan from the Hill Country town of Fredericksburg, with a calm temper. The main weapon in that fleet was the aircraft carrierAIR-kraft KAIR-ee-erA warship with a long flat deck from which airplanes take off and land., a ship with a long flat deck from which planes can take off and land. Pearl Harbor had destroyed American battleships but had missed the carriers, and now they were the heart of the American fleet. Nimitz had another advantage. A team of navy codebreakers in Hawaii, led by Commander Joseph Rochefort and working in a windowless basement, had broken part of the Japanese naval code. They learned that Japan planned an attack on a target called "AF." To prove that AF was Midway, a small island in the middle of the Pacific, they had the island send out a false message that its water supply was low. Soon after, the Japanese reported that "AF" was short of water.

⚡ DECIDE FIRST

What might have happened if Admiral NimitzCHES-ter NIM-itsThe admiral who commanded the American Pacific Fleet. had guessed wrong and sent his carriers somewhere else? Decide first and jot down two results before you open this.
The aircraft carriersAIR-kraft KAIR-ee-erA warship with a long flat deck from which airplanes take off and land. were the heart of the American fleet. With them elsewhere, Midway might have fallen with little fight, and the Japanese fleet might have kept its strength. But might is the word. Nobody can know. This is reasoning from what we know, not a certain result.

One more piece of luck and hard work stood behind the plan. A month earlier, in the Coral Sea, off Australia, American and Japanese carriers had fought the first naval battle in which the ships never came within sight of each other, because the planes did all the fighting. The carrier Yorktown was badly hurt. Experts guessed that she would need three months in a shipyard, but Nimitz gave the yard workers three days, and she sailed again for Midway. The American task forces were commanded by two admirals, one of them Raymond Spruance, a calm man who took charge when the usual commander fell ill with a skin disease.

Nimitz sent his three carriers to wait. On the morning of June 4, the Japanese fleet, led by Admiral Yamamoto's four large carriers, began to attack Midway. The first American planes to find the Japanese ships were slow torpedo planes, and they were nearly wiped out. In one squadron of fifteen planes, only one pilot survived. Yet their attacks pulled the Japanese fighters down close to the waves, and the high sky above was left empty. The American planes found the Japanese carriers at a moment when their decks were crowded with planes being refueled and rearmed. Within minutes, American dive bombers destroyed three of the four. The fourth carrier was sunk later that day. The Americans lost one carrier, the damaged Yorktown, which a submarine sank two days later. Japan lost four, along with many of its most skilled pilots, and could not replace them. In April 1943, American codebreakers learned the flight plan of Admiral Yamamoto himself, and fighters shot down his plane over the Solomon Islands. Midway was a victory of intelligence, of courage, and of luck. A small delay one way or the other might have changed it all. Historians who like to study contingency often start here.

After Midway, the Americans went on the offense. In August 1942, Marines landed on Guadalcanalgwah-dul-kuh-NALThe island in the Solomons where American Marines landed in August 1942 and won in February 1943, Japan's first big defeat on land., an island in the Solomon chain where the Japanese were building an airfield. The Marines finished it and named it Henderson Field, for a pilot killed at Midway. One of the Marines who earned the Medal of Honor there was Sergeant John Basilone, who would die on a later beach. For six months, soldiers, sailors, and airmen fought on land, at sea, and in the air. Australian coastwatchers hiding on nearby islands radioed warnings when Japanese planes and ships approached. Japanese ships brought reinforcements by night, and so many ships were sunk in the waters off the island that sailors called them Ironbottom Sound. The fighting was brutal, and the jungle added disease and misery. More than seven thousand Americans died there, and about thirty thousand Japanese, many of them from hunger and disease. The Americans held the airfield and won in February 1943. It was Japan's first big defeat on land.

From there, the Americans developed a plan known as island hoppingEYE-lund HOP-ingThe American strategy of taking some Pacific islands and skipping others, which left Japanese troops on the skipped islands cut off.. Two drives moved at once. Nimitz's ships and Marines advanced across the central Pacific, while MacArthur's soldiers worked up the coast of New Guinea toward the Philippines. There were too many Japanese-held islands to capture them all. Instead, the Americans seized some, built airfields on them, and skipped others, leaving the Japanese troops there cut off and useless. Meanwhile, American submarines, though only a small part of the Navy, sank more than half of Japan's merchant ships, cutting off the oil and supplies that its war depended on. In June 1944, in the Philippine Sea, American pilots shot down hundreds of Japanese planes in a single day, in a fight the pilots called the Marianas Turkey Shoot. The Americans' own planes returned at dusk, low on fuel, far from their ships. Admiral Marc Mitscher ordered every light on the carriers turned on to guide them home, even though submarines might be near. The factories at home, which you will read about next week, poured out ships, planes, and tanks. American forces moved west across the Pacific, one step at a time. In November 1943, at Tarawa, more than a thousand Marines died in a few days taking a tiny island. The tide was lower than expected, and many landing boats grounded on a coral reef far from shore, so the men had to wade for hundreds of yards under fire. The lessons from that battle helped the Navy and Marines plan better. Later landings used tracked boats that could climb over reefs, and the bombardment before them grew longer and heavier.

⚡ HOW MUCH DID CHANCE MATTER?
At Midway, the Americans had skill, good intelligence, and some luck. How much did luck matter?

Many Americans served in this war who did not usually appear in the story. Navajo men served in the Marine Corps as code talkers. Using their own language as the base of a code that the Japanese could not break, they sent important messages quickly and accurately. About four hundred Navajo served in this way. Florida also played its part. The Naval Air Station at Pensacola trained thousands of the pilots who flew from the carriers, and bases across the state trained soldiers and sailors for both oceans. Doolittle's raiders had practiced their short takeoffs at Eglin Field, in Florida's panhandle, before they sailed.

By the summer of 1944, American forces were close enough to Japan to attack it directly. After they took the island of Saipan in June, at a cost of about three thousand American lives, and after thousands of Japanese civilians there died, many by leaping from cliffs rather than surrender, giant B-29 bombers, the biggest bombers of the war, could fly from there to Tokyo. In October, MacArthur kept his promise. He waded ashore in the Philippines on the island of Leyte, and he broadcast to the Filipinos that he had returned. A few days later, the largest naval battle in history, the Battle of Leyte GulfBAT-ul uv LAY-tee gulfThe October 1944 naval battle near the Philippines, the largest in history, which destroyed most of Japan's navy., destroyed most of what was left of Japan's navy, including four carriers and three battleships. The Japanese plan was to lure the American carriers away with a decoy fleet, and for a time it worked, leaving a strait open to the Japanese battleships. They turned back, and the landing was not harmed. It also saw the first organized attacks by pilots who flew their planes into American ships on purpose. They were called kamikaze, which means "divine wind."

The last stop before Japan itself was Iwo JimaEE-woh JEE-muhA small volcanic island, about 750 miles from Tokyo, that the Marines captured in February and March of 1945., a small volcanic island only about 750 miles from Tokyo. American leaders wanted its airfields so that fighter planes could protect the bombers and damaged planes could land safely. The Marines landed on February 19, 1945. The Japanese defenders had dug deep into the rock. They had built hundreds of hidden tunnels and bunkers, and they planned to make the Americans pay for every yard. Their general, Tadamichi Kuribayashi, had ordered each man to kill ten Americans before he died, and he forbade the wild charges that had cost Japan so many men on earlier islands. The defenders held their fire until the beach was crowded with Marines. The beaches were made of black volcanic ash, and the Marines sank to their ankles and their vehicles bogged down. They did pay. Navajo code talkers sent hundreds of messages during the first days without a mistake. In five weeks of fighting, about 6,800 Americans died, and almost all of the roughly 21,000 Japanese defenders were killed. On the fifth day, Marines raised a flag on Mount Suribachi. A photographer, Joe Rosenthal, took a picture of a second, larger flag-raising that day. It became one of the most famous images in American history. It won the Pulitzer Prize that year and later became the model for a national memorial. The Secretary of the Navy, James Forrestal, watched the first flag go up from the beach and told a Marine general that it meant a Marine Corps for another five hundred years. He asked for that first flag as a keepsake, which is why a second, larger one was raised. Three of the six men in it were killed in the fighting that followed. One of the survivors, Ira Hayes, was a Pima Indian from Arizona.

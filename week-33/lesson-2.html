📖 TODAY'S LESSON

In the early hours of June 17, 1972, a security guard named Frank Wills noticed a piece of tape holding a door lock open at the WatergateWAW-ter-gaytThe scandal that began with a 1972 break-in at Democratic Party offices in the Watergate complex and ended with Nixon's resignation., an office and apartment complex in Washington. He had taken one piece of tape off earlier that night. Now there was another. He called the police. They arrested five men inside the offices of the Democratic National Committee. The burglars carried cameras and listening equipment, and they had a thick roll of crisp hundred-dollar bills with numbers in order. One of the five, James McCord, was a former CIA officer who worked as the security chief for Nixon's campaign. Nixon's press secretary called the event "a third-rate burglary attempt." In the months that followed, it became clear that it was something far larger.

The men were linked to the Committee to Re-elect the President, Nixon's campaign group. Money from the campaign had paid for the burglary. The serial numbers on the hundred-dollar bills were traced to a Miami bank account, and from there the trail led to the campaign's own funds. The burglary was part of a wider set of illegal acts, often called "dirty tricks," against the president's political opponents, and it had been planned by two men with ties to the White House and the campaign, E. Howard Hunt and G. Gordon Liddy. Nixon denied any part in the break-in, and he won reelection in a landslide a few months later. But two young reporters at the Washington Post, Bob Woodward and Carl Bernstein, kept digging, helped by a secret source inside the government. They called him Deep Throat, and they kept his name secret for more than thirty years. In 2005 he was revealed to be Mark Felt, who had been a top official of the FBI. Meanwhile, Judge John Sirica, who presided over the burglars' trial, pressed the defendants to talk. He handed down very long provisional sentences and hinted that they might be cut for anyone who cooperated. In March 1973, McCord wrote a letter admitting that political pressure had been put on the burglars to keep quiet, and Sirica read it aloud in court.

The cover-up now began to crack. On March 21, 1973, Nixon's lawyer, John Dean, told the president that a cancer was growing on the presidency, and that paying the burglars to keep quiet could not go on forever. Other men were beginning to talk to prosecutors, and soon Dean was too. On April 30, Nixon went on television. He said that his two closest aides, H. R. Haldeman and John Ehrlichman, had resigned, and that Dean had been dismissed. He accepted responsibility, he said, but he denied knowing about the cover-up.

The question was now whether the president had known. The Senate set up a special committee, led by Senator Sam Ervin of North Carolina, a student of the Constitution, and the hearings were shown on television during the summer of 1973. Millions of Americans watched. Senator Howard Baker of Tennessee, a Republican, kept coming back to one question: what did the president know, and when did he know it? Nixon's former counsel, John Dean, testified that the president had taken part in a cover-up. It was his word against the president's. Then, in July, in a quiet moment, a former White House aide named Alexander Butterfield answered a routine question from a committee lawyer and revealed that Nixon had a secret taping system in the Oval Office. Microphones switched on by voice, and the system had been installed in 1971. Every conversation was on tape. The tapes could settle the matter, and everyone knew it.

So did the special prosecutorSPESH-ul PROS-ih-kyoo-terA lawyer appointed to investigate possible crimes by government officials without taking orders from the people being investigated., Archibald Cox, a Harvard law professor who had served in Kennedy's administration, and who had been appointed by the Justice Department to look into the case without taking orders from the president. Nixon refused. He claimed executive privilegeig-ZEK-yuh-tiv PRIV-uh-lijThe claim that a president may keep some conversations and records secret in order to receive honest advice., the idea that a president may keep some conversations secret in order to receive honest advice. In October he offered a compromise: an older senator he trusted, John Stennis of Mississippi, would listen to the tapes and report what was on them. Cox said no. A summary from a friendly listener was not evidence. Then, on October 20, 1973, Nixon ordered the attorney general to fire Cox. The attorney general, Elliot Richardson, resigned rather than obey. His deputy, William Ruckelshaus, refused too and was fired. The order was finally carried out by the next man in line, Robert Bork. The press called these events the Saturday Night MassacreSAT-er-day NITE MASS-uh-kerThe night of October 20, 1973, when Nixon ordered the special prosecutor fired, and the attorney general and his deputy resigned rather than obey..

Public anger was huge, and the pressure on Nixon grew stronger. A flood of telegrams poured into Washington, and in the House of Representatives members began to talk openly about removing him. In November, Nixon told a room full of newspaper editors that he was not a crook. Then his lawyers admitted that one of the tapes had a gap of eighteen and a half minutes, in a conversation held three days after the break-in. His secretary, Rose Mary Woods, said she had erased part of it by accident. Experts later said that it had been erased by hand in several separate sessions. Meanwhile Nixon appointed a new special prosecutor, Leon Jaworski, who also sued for the tapes.

The case went to the Supreme Court. Jaworski had asked for the tapes of sixty-four conversations, and Nixon's lawyers argued that a president alone decides what privilege covers. In United States v. Nixon, decided in July 1974, only sixteen days after the arguments, the Court ruled unanimously, eight to zero, that executive privilege exists but is not absolute. When a president's claim of secrecy collides with the need for evidence in a criminal case, the Court held, the claim must give way. Chief Justice Warren Burger, whom Nixon himself had appointed, wrote the opinion. There were eight votes instead of nine because Justice William Rehnquist, who had worked in Nixon's Justice Department, took no part. You will read part of the decision this week. It built on the principle you met earlier in the course, in Marbury v. Madison: the Court says what the law is, even to the president. This was a clear victory for the idea of a government of laws and not of men. It is worth noticing, though, that Nixon had a choice. He could have defied the Court, and then no army and no police officer would have forced him. What stopped him was the force of custom, public opinion, and the fear that Congress would act.

Congress was already acting. In late July 1974, the House Judiciary Committee voted on impeachmentim-PEECH-muntThe process by which the House of Representatives accuses an official of "high crimes and misdemeanors" so that the Senate can try the official., which is the process by which the House of Representatives accuses an officer of "high crimes and misdemeanors" so that the Senate can try him. The committee approved three articles, including obstruction of justiceub-STRUK-shun uv JUS-tisThe crime of illegally blocking or interfering with an investigation or a court case., meaning illegal efforts to block an investigation. Members from both parties voted for them. The chairman, Peter Rodino of New Jersey, kept the debates orderly and slow, and they were carried live on television. The vote on the first article was twenty-seven to eleven, and six Republicans were among the twenty-seven.

Then the Court ordered the tapes released. One of them, recorded six days after the break-in, showed Nixon ordering aides to tell the FBI to stop looking into the burglary for reasons of national security. It was the "smoking gun." It was made public on August 5, and even the committee's remaining Republican defenders of Nixon said that they would now vote against him. Two days later, Nixon's own supporters in Congress came to the Oval Office to tell him he would be convicted. They were Senator Barry Goldwater and the Republican leaders of the House and Senate, John Rhodes and Hugh Scott. Goldwater said that Nixon could count on no more than about fifteen votes in the Senate. On the evening of August 8, 1974, Nixon announced his resignation on television. The next day he said goodbye to his staff in an emotional speech, walked to a helicopter on the White House lawn, and left Washington. At the door he turned and raised both arms in a V. He remains the only American president ever to resign.

    Vice President Gerald FordJAIR-uld FORDThe 38th president. He became vice president in 1973 and president when Nixon resigned in 1974. took the oath. Ford was a decent and well-liked man from Michigan who had been appointed vice president only the year before, after Spiro Agnew resigned in a separate scandal over bribes. Ford had spent twenty-five years in the House of Representatives, and he became the first president who had never been elected either president or vice president. He told the nation that "our long national nightmare is over." A month later, he gave Nixon a full pardonPAR-dunA president's power to forgive a person for a federal crime and prevent charges or punishment., which is the power of a president to forgive a crime and to prevent charges. He announced it on television on a Sunday morning, and it covered any federal crime Nixon had committed or might have committed in office. Ford said he wanted to spare the country a long and painful trial. Many Americans were furious, and they suspected a secret deal, though no evidence of one was ever found. His press secretary resigned in protest, his approval ratings fell sharply, and in October he went before a House committee to explain himself. The pardon probably cost Ford the election of 1976.

    That election was won by Jimmy CarterJIM-ee KAR-terThe 39th president, from 1977 to 1981. He was a former governor of Georgia and a naval officer., a former governor of Georgia and a naval officer, who ran as an outsider and promised never to lie to the American people. He had grown up on a peanut farm near Plains, Georgia, and in the years after Watergate, many voters were ready to trust a plain-spoken man from a small town. His term was hard. Inflation and unemployment stayed high, and long lines for gas returned in 1979. Yet Carter also had a real success. In September 1978, he brought Egypt's president, Anwar Sadat, and Israel's prime minister, Menachem Begin, to Camp David, the presidential retreat in Maryland. After nearly two weeks of talks, with Carter carrying proposals back and forth between their cabins, the two men agreed on a framework for peace, and the next year Egypt and Israel signed a treaty. It was the first time an Arab country had made peace with Israel.

    ⚡ HOW MUCH DID THE RULING DO?
    The Supreme Court ordered the tapes released, but Nixon could have refused. How much did the ruling by itself make him obey?

    In July of 1979, Carter had planned a speech on energy and then canceled it. Instead he went back to Camp David for ten days of meetings, and he invited governors, ministers, business leaders, and ordinary citizens to tell him what was wrong. Then he gave a television speech that surprised the nation. He said that America faced a "crisis of confidence," and that too many people had turned toward self-indulgence and consumption. He also proposed limits on oil imports. Some praised his honesty. Others thought a president should offer hope and a plan, and they judged the speech a sign of weakness. Reporters later called it the "malaise" speech, though Carter never used that word. Two days later he asked every member of his cabinet to submit a resignation, and he accepted five. Within months, American hostages were taken in Iran, and the hard times worsened. You will read the speech this week.

    The aftermath changed the law. Many of Nixon's closest aides went to prison, among them Haldeman, Ehrlichman, and the former attorney general, John Mitchell. In 1974, Congress rewrote the rules for campaign money, setting limits on contributions and creating the Federal Election Commission. That same year it strengthened the Freedom of Information Act, which lets citizens ask to see government records, over Ford's veto. In 1978 it passed a law that set up a way to appoint outside prosecutors to investigate high officials.

    So did the system work? One answer is yes. The press, the courts, the Congress, and public opinion all played their part, and a powerful president was forced from office without violence. Another answer is that the system came very close to failing. It took a lucky break, the discovery of the tapes, and the courage of a few officials. A classical republican would say that such a system depends on the habits of the people who work within it. Laws alone did not defeat the cover-up. Character did, and that includes the character of the men who refused to obey Nixon's order to fire Cox. You will weigh this question in this week's discussion.
